# The Requirement Belongs to the Chapter Next Door

## Pattern

Working the authentication lane. The model shows a login, and the description says an administrator can view any customer's order history from a support console. The authorization chapter is in the selected level and is another lane's work.

## Considered

Filing a ruling in this lane that the support console lets one identity read another's data, against an access-control requirement.

## Ruling

Rejected — not because the concern is wrong, but because it is not this chapter's.

## Why

One lane is one chapter, and the chapters partition the standard. A ruling filed under the wrong one lands in the wrong summary, the wrong coverage row, and the wrong place for a reader following the standard's structure. The service will not correct it: the chapter comes from the lane the agent is running, so a claim reaching past its chapter is filed under this chapter with another chapter's subject.

The boundary is stated in the chapter itself. Proving *who* somebody is belongs here. Deciding what they may *do* belongs to authorization. A support console reading other people's records is entirely the second question.

The right move is to file nothing here and let the authorization lane rule on it. That lane sees the same model and the same description; nothing is lost by staying inside the boundary, and a duplicate ruling in two chapters is worse than one in the right place.

What *would* be this lane's is the console's own login — whether administrative access is authenticated more strongly than a customer's, which is a factor question rather than a permission one.

## What decided it

The chapter boundary the lane's own scope states, applied against the subject of the concern. The concern was real; the lane was wrong.
